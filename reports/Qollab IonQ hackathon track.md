# Register Thursday, Then Win the Run Button

All of the prize money at Georgia Tech Qiskit Fall Fest 2026 is in the **Qollab × IonQ track**. That track is the Georgia Tech node of a global 48-hour online hackathon running **Friday Oct 9, 7:00 PM ET to Sunday Oct 11, 7:00 PM ET**. The one deadline you can still miss is **Qollab registration, which closes tomorrow, Thursday Oct 8 at 11:00 AM ET**. It is separate from the Georgia Tech sign-up form, and you need both. Georgia Tech's student Quantum Computing Association runs the festival as part of IBM's Qiskit Fall Fest. The track's rules, judging and prizes belong to Qollab, a browser-based quantum coding platform operated by fluxDev, Inc., and its partner IonQ. Global first place pays **US$10,000 cash plus US$20,000 in compute credits**. Six specialty awards pay US$500 cash plus US$5,000 in credits each. Georgia Tech's local prizes exist only if the node gets ten or more eligible entries. Every entry must be a Qiskit program, published and runnable on Qollab under the MIT license, with a project page and a 2–3 minute public video. Judges score four criteria with no published weights: Novelty & Creativity, Technical Execution, Impact Potential and Documentation Quality. They do it by pressing Run on a free simulator, and IonQ hardware results earn no points. Five years of IonQ hackathon winners, plus Qollab's own past winners, suggest a consistent formula. Build a small, fast circuit whose quantum behavior is essential to the idea. Compare it honestly against classical or noisy baselines in a way that shows you understand what IonQ's trapped-ion hardware does well. Document it well enough that a stranger can run and extend it in minutes. This is the event's first edition, so there are no past local winners to study. As of October 7, no judges, rubric weights or Georgia Tech-specific problem statements have been published.

## Six hard requirements and a tutorial bar gate every entry

Qollab's submission rules turn the hackathon into a software-publishing contest as much as a quantum one. Every project must use **Qiskit**. It must follow **UI accessibility best practices** and **run on Qollab's free simulators, including IonQ's simulator**. It must be **published and runnable on qollab.xyz under the MIT license before the deadline**. It must have a **Qollab project page** explaining what it does, what problem it explores and how to run it, in enough depth to understand, replicate or extend it. It must be **built during the build window**. And it must include a **2–3 minute demo video at a public link that opens without sign-in** ([Qollab Rules & terms](https://qollab.xyz/programs/hackathon/terms)). The video can be a screen recording with voiceover hosted on YouTube, Loom, Vimeo or Google Drive with anyone-with-link access; you do not need to appear on camera ([Qollab FAQ](https://qollab.xyz/programs/hackathon/how-it-works#faq)). Participant obligations add a required attribution line, "This effort is supported by Qollab & IonQ". They also require keeping the project on Qollab for 12 months and providing a payment method in case you win cash ([Qollab Rules & terms](https://qollab.xyz/programs/hackathon/terms)). The GT site condenses all of this into a six-item checklist: solo or up to four people, the Georgia Tech node, built in the window, published runnable on Qollab, Qiskit on IonQ's simulator, and an MIT license ([GT site tracks](https://qhackathon-gt.lovable.app/#tracks)). Where the two differ, Qollab's longer list governs.

The judging workflow explains why "runnable" is enforced so strictly. **Judges open the project page, read the code and press Run.** An external frontend or backend is allowed, but that repository must be public, require no sign-in, carry the MIT license, list its dependencies and contain no secrets ([Qollab FAQ](https://qollab.xyz/programs/hackathon/how-it-works#faq)). The captain submits from the Qollab registration page any time between Friday 7 PM and Sunday 7 PM ET and **can keep editing until the deadline**. After it, entries can be withdrawn but not edited or added. **Partial or rough projects remain eligible** ([Qollab FAQ](https://qollab.xyz/programs/hackathon/how-it-works#faq)). That combination makes an early placeholder submission close to free insurance.

The most consequential rule is the **tutorial bar**. Each submission must go beyond a reference implementation or tutorial exercise. It must show at least one of three things. The first is a novel application or problem context. The second is a technical extension, and Qollab's examples are noise or error mitigation, parameter optimization and comparative benchmarking. The third is an original analytical or educational contribution. Panels may score a project that fails this bar **zero on both Novelty & Creativity and Technical Execution** ([Qollab Rules & terms](https://qollab.xyz/programs/hackathon/terms)). That wipes out half the rubric. A polished Grover demo or a textbook QAOA on a toy graph is a losing entry no matter how good its interface looks.

The originality rules are permissive but strict about disclosure. Projects must be substantially new work from the window. Qollab explicitly welcomes AI tools, open-source libraries, prior tutorials and Qollab templates as starting points, as long as they are disclosed, and it judges what the team adds. The project page must state what existed before the window and credit any forks, templates, repositories or tutorials used. Grounds for disqualification include passing off a pre-existing project as new, copying code, circuits or text without attribution, using a template without disclosure, and multiple teams submitting substantially identical projects ([Qollab Rules & terms](https://qollab.xyz/programs/hackathon/terms); [Qollab FAQ](https://qollab.xyz/programs/hackathon/how-it-works#faq)). The GT terms add that participants remain responsible for the accuracy and attribution of AI-generated material ([GT T&C §5, §7](https://qhackathon-gt.lovable.app/terms-and-conditions)). In practice you can learn the platform, choose a dataset and plan the architecture before Friday. Write the project code inside the window, and disclose anything else.

Ownership stays with you, but openness is mandatory. The GT terms leave IP with its creators ([GT T&C §6](https://qhackathon-gt.lovable.app/terms-and-conditions)). The MIT license and the 12-month hosting requirement mean your project will stay public and forkable. Winners must also let Qollab and IonQ feature their work, handle their own tax reporting, and post at least once on social media about taking part ([Qollab Rules & terms](https://qollab.xyz/programs/hackathon/terms)). Scoring decisions are final. Only a disqualification can be appealed, in writing to hello@qollab.xyz within 24 hours, and a reviewer outside the panel decides within five business days. Awards withdrawn through disqualification are not reassigned to other teams, and paid awards must be returned within 30 days if a disqualification comes later. A platform or simulator outage longer than 12 hours can trigger a proportional extension or late submissions with an outage-impact statement; shorter outages do not ([Qollab Rules & terms](https://qollab.xyz/programs/hackathon/terms)). The GT terms add that hardware and API access is not guaranteed, and that participants are responsible for their own backups ([GT T&C §12, §16](https://qhackathon-gt.lovable.app/terms-and-conditions)). Georgia Tech's Code of Conduct covers every space, Slack and submission platforms included. It bans harassment, doxxing and unauthorized use of others' accounts or compute, requires crediting teammates and sources, and allows penalties up to disqualification, bans from QCA events or referral to Georgia Tech authorities ([GT Code of Conduct](https://qhackathon-gt.lovable.app/code-of-conduct)).

## Four unweighted criteria and a Run button decide the money

Every Qollab × IonQ submission is scored on **Novelty & Creativity, Technical Execution, Impact Potential and Documentation Quality**. Local judges score first, and the top projects advance to a global panel that re-scores them with the same rubric ([Qollab How it works: Judging](https://qollab.xyz/programs/hackathon/how-it-works#judging)). **No weights, point scales or sub-criteria are published.** The local panel combines a faculty or staff judge with Qollab and IonQ representatives, and nobody on it has been named ([Qollab FAQ](https://qollab.xyz/programs/hackathon/how-it-works#faq)). Judges run every project on a free simulator, and **whether you used hardware or a simulator is not scored**: IonQ simulator and hardware results neither add nor subtract points ([Qollab Rules & terms](https://qollab.xyz/programs/hackathon/terms)). Georgia Tech's local judging happens the week of October 12 and does not require participants to attend ([GT site schedule](https://qhackathon-gt.lovable.app/#schedule)).

How projects move from the node to the global round matters a great deal for strategy. If a node receives **fewer than ten projects**, the panel evaluates all of them and sends the **top three** to global judging without paying local prizes. With ten or more, the top three receive local awards and all three still advance. In both cases a node can also forward up to **one project per global specialty award** ([Qollab Rules & terms](https://qollab.xyz/programs/hackathon/terms)). For a GT team, the real first gate is **a top-three finish at Georgia Tech, or being the node's pick for a specialty category**.

Three things follow from the scoring design. First, with no published weights, the safest assumption is roughly equal weighting. The closest IonQ precedent, the 2022 iQuHACK Microsoft and IonQ rubric, weighted each of its five criteria at exactly 20% ([iQuHACK 2022 scoring rubric](https://docs.google.com/document/d/e/2PACX-1vR5PVoInN_Fi42lIOchhblgGBPblgNyouj1XHukonZ4sdqY-p5ulS9TxdzvddEcDNFc5k_6teFyKzXv/pub)). A balanced project beats one that maxes out a single criterion. Second, **Documentation Quality is both a quarter of the rubric and a standalone specialty award**, and it is the criterion most fully under your control in the final hours. Third, you declare a theme at submission and can change it until the deadline. Off-theme projects can still place but cannot win theme awards ([Qollab FAQ](https://qollab.xyz/programs/hackathon/how-it-works#faq)). Always declare one.

How many teams the GT node will draw is unknown. The club's LinkedIn page has about 339 followers, and this is a first-year event ([LinkedIn: Quantum Computing @ GT](https://www.linkedin.com/company/georgiatech-quantum/)), so fewer than ten GT entries is plausible. In that case there would be no local cash, but the top three would go straight to the global round. The opposite case also deserves weight: Georgia Tech is large, the event is free, and a US$10,000 headline prize draws attention. Plan to be in the top three either way.

## Qollab runs Qiskit inside a browser tab, with sharp edges

Qollab describes itself as a community effort to make quantum computing accessible to builders of every background ([Qollab About](https://qollab.xyz/about)). IonQ lists its features as a no-setup browser playground for quantum code, code sharing with forking and remixing, showcases of signature experiments, and coding challenges and tutorials ([IonQ newsroom](https://www.ionq.com/news/ionq-partners-with-qollabs-creative-challenge-advancing-creative-quantum-innovation)). It is still small: **35 contributors, 88 projects and 7 forks**, with 51 of those projects published by the Qollab team itself ([Qollab Contributors](https://qollab.xyz/contributors)). Its GitHub organization holds patched forks of Qiskit and qiskit-ionq adapted for an in-browser Python runtime, plus QAVE, a quantum algorithm visualization engine ([GitHub qollabxyz](https://github.com/qollabxyz)). Alongside the hackathon, Qollab runs a rolling **Grant Program** that pays up to US$20,000 in IonQ compute credits for porting, forking or publishing open-source quantum work on the platform ([Qollab Programs](https://qollab.xyz/programs)). Because the catalog is so small, the Qollab representatives on the panel almost certainly know every existing project on it. That matters for novelty, as discussed below.

The runtime is unusual, and it shapes what you can build. **Python runs on Pyodide (WebAssembly) inside the browser**, and JavaScript projects run in the page. No code runs on a server except the quantum job itself, which goes to IonQ's servers only when a remote backend is selected. Qiskit is always installed; if `requirements.txt` does not name it, the runtime adds `qiskit>=2.5.2,<3.0.0`. These libraries install with one click:

| Library | Version |
|---|---|

| qiskit-optimization | 0.7.0 |
| qiskit-nature (with pyscf) | 0.8.0 |
| qiskit-finance | 0.4.1 |
| qiskit-machine-learning | 0.9.1 |

| PQCA (brings qiskit_aer and qiskit_ibm_runtime) | qiskit_aer 0.17.2, qiskit_ibm_runtime 0.50.0 |

Pure-Python wheels and Pyodide-built packages also install, and NumPy, SciPy and Matplotlib load automatically ([Qollab docs: Runtime environment](https://qollab.xyz/learn/docs/runtime-environment)).

The sharp edges follow from that design. First, the runtime **injects the `backend` object**, and whoever presses Run chooses it, so you must not create or hardcode a backend. Second, **IonQ backends accept one job per run**. Everything an IonQ run measures must therefore go into one circuit or into a single `backend.run` call. Third, network access is **GET-only to CORS-enabled servers**. POST requests, WebSockets and workers are blocked. Fourth, **nothing persists between runs**, so saved state must ship as project files, and `input()` does not work. Fifth, if `main()` has typed parameters (int, float, str or bool), Run displays a form for them. Sixth, an `index.html` plus `main.js` turns the project into a rendered JavaScript page that can call Python. Finally, the limits are **100 files of 2 MB each and 200 MB per account**, with no time limit on runs ([Qollab docs: Runtime environment](https://qollab.xyz/learn/docs/runtime-environment); [Qollab FAQ](https://qollab.xyz/programs/hackathon/how-it-works#faq)). Your code is public, so API keys must never appear in it.

Qollab warns that IonQ noise simulation runs once per shot, so run time grows with the shot count, and runs near the qubit ceiling can still fail ([Qollab docs: Compute backends](https://qollab.xyz/learn/docs/compute-backends)). Hardware runs show a cost estimate first and ask for confirmation above an allowance that defaults to one-tenth of your credit balance. Cost depends on the compiled 1-qubit and 2-qubit gate counts plus shots, and any gate acting on N qubits is billed as 6(N−2) two-qubit gates ([Qollab docs: How credits work](https://qollab.xyz/learn/docs/how-credits-work)). **No hackathon credit allowance for hardware during the build was found**, and the GT checklist only mentions IonQ's simulator ([GT site tracks](https://qhackathon-gt.lovable.app/#tracks)). There is **no official starter repository**. Qollab points entrants to its six-lesson course *Programming your first quantum circuits*, whose last lesson covers noise and real hardware. It also offers forkable concept explainers on QAOA (up to a 24-node graph), VQE for hydrogen, interference and Grover search, and entanglement ([Qollab Learn](https://qollab.xyz/learn)). Qollab's FAQ cites two forks, *QCFlows: Multi-file Example* and *Quantum Hackathon Soundtrack*, as examples of properly disclosed starting code ([Qollab FAQ](https://qollab.xyz/programs/hackathon/how-it-works#faq)).

Four engineering consequences follow; these are my inferences. First, the judge's Run click executes inside a browser tab. Suppose the built-in simulator keeps a full statevector. A 24-qubit run would then need about **268 MB** just for amplitudes (2^24 complex numbers × 16 bytes). Design the default run for well under the ceiling, around 20 qubits or fewer, finishing in seconds. Second, because the judge picks the backend, the code should read the injected backend's capacity and handle whatever it gets, rather than assuming one. Third, nothing persists between runs, so precompute IonQ Forte noise-model results during the build. Commit them as JSON files, and have the page show ideal, IonQ-noisy and IBM-noisy results side by side even when the judge only runs the built-in simulator. Fourth, PQCA brings qiskit_aer into the runtime, and IonQ publishes depolarizing parameters for its older Forte 1 noise model. With both, you can build a client-side approximate IonQ noise model that runs live on the free local simulator ([IonQ Docs: noise models](https://docs.ionq.com/features/simulation-with-noise-models)). Label it as approximate, because IonQ says its current Forte models are more complex and not yet documented.

### Native ZZ makes every Ising-type term a single two-qubit gate

On Forte the native gates are GPi(φ) and GPi2(φ) on single qubits plus a tunable two-qubit **ZZ(θ) = exp(−iπθ Z⊗Z)**. All parameters are in turns, where one turn is 2π. Z rotations are virtual and cost nothing. Circuits written in native gates skip IonQ's compiler and optimizer, have debiasing off by default, and cannot mix native and abstract gates ([IonQ Docs: native gates](https://docs.ionq.com/features/getting-started-with-native-gates)). In Qiskit, `qiskit_ionq` exposes `GPIGate`, `GPI2Gate` and `ZZGate`, and `get_backend(..., gateset="native")` transpiles to them. IonQ warns that Qiskit optimization levels 2 and 3 can apply passes that conflict with IonQ's own compiler, so you should transpile at **level 0 or 1**. Operations such as `UnitaryGate`, `u` and `iswap` raise `IonQGateError` unless they are transpiled first ([IonQ Docs: Qiskit native gates](https://docs.ionq.com/sdks/qiskit/native-gates-qiskit)). Working from the gate definitions, a Qiskit `RZZ(φ)` equals native ZZ with θ = φ/2π turns. That means each ZZ cost term in a QAOA or Ising circuit costs **one native two-qubit gate instead of two CNOTs**, and all-to-all connectivity means no SWAPs. Multi-controlled gates are the opposite: an N-qubit gate counts as 6(N−2) two-qubit gates ([IonQ Docs: Job cost](https://docs.ionq.com/guides/job-cost-and-usage)). IonQ itself recommends abstract gates for general algorithm runs, because its compiler usually does better. Hand-written native circuits are worth it only when the gate-level behavior is the point of the project ([IonQ Docs: native gates](https://docs.ionq.com/features/getting-started-with-native-gates)).

### Noise models are free, but debiasing never runs in simulation

The cloud simulator offers an **ideal** model (the default) plus **forte-1** (updated September 2025), **forte-enterprise-1** (added September 2025) and the legacy aria-1 and aria-2 models; the Harmony model has been removed. Forte-class models are capped at **29 qubits** and Aria models at 25. Noisy simulation runs once per shot, can be seeded, and is free. IonQ documents only its older single-channel depolarizing models; for the older Forte 1 model, the single-qubit error rate is about 0.000267 and the two-qubit rate about 0.004949. IonQ describes the September 2025 Forte models as more complex, with documentation still being prepared, and gives no accuracy guarantee ([IonQ Docs: noise models](https://docs.ionq.com/features/simulation-with-noise-models)). IonQ's built-in mitigation is **debiasing**. It splits a job of 500 or more shots into 25 equivalent circuit variants and combines the results by averaging, or by "sharpening", a plurality vote you choose when retrieving results. It is on by default for abstract-gate jobs of 500 or more shots and off for native-gate jobs. It is **unavailable on every IonQ simulator, including the noisy ones** ([IonQ Docs: Debiasing](https://docs.ionq.com/features/error-mitigation-debiasing)). The unreleased main branch of qiskit-ionq replaces `error_mitigation=` and `sharpen=` with `debiasing=`, `symmetry_verification=` and an `aggregation=` option that includes a new "dnl" filter ([qiskit-ionq README](https://github.com/qiskit-community/qiskit-ionq/blob/main/README.md)). For a simulator-judged hackathon, any mitigation you show has to run on your side: constraint or symmetry post-selection, or zero-noise extrapolation.

### Hard limits shape what fits in one job

The IonQ API accepts 1 to 1,000,000 shots. A multi-circuit job can hold up to 5,000 circuits and 150,000 gates, and the request body is capped at 10 MB ([IonQ API v0.4: Create job](https://docs.ionq.com/api-reference/v0.4/jobs/create-job)). **Dynamic circuits** (if/else, while, for, switch) are **unsupported on every IonQ target, simulator included, and IonQ does not plan them for any live system**. Mid-circuit measurement and reset are available on some targets through OpenQASM 3 ([IonQ API v0.4: OpenQASM 3](https://docs.ionq.com/api-reference/v0.4/openqasm3)). Teleportation and error-correction demos therefore need deferred measurement or classical post-selection if they are going to run on IonQ's simulator.

Fidelity sets a softer limit. A simple model where fidelity falls by a factor of (1 − e) per two-qubit gate puts the Forte 1 noise model (e ≈ 0.00495) at **50% fidelity after about 140 two-qubit gates** and at 1/e after about 200. At the independently measured Forte Enterprise error (e ≈ 0.0085), those points arrive at about 80 and 117 gates. Budget **about 100 two-qubit gates** for tasks that depend on expectation values or full distributions. Tasks that only need the right answer to appear in the samples tolerate far more. Peaked-output optimization on IonQ hardware has stayed competitive at about 1,000 two-qubit gates on 32 qubits ([arXiv:2604.26834](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.26834)), and flag-qubit mitigation has supported benchmarking circuits of up to 2,356 two-qubit gates on Forte Enterprise ([arXiv:2506.05757](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.05757)). Signal does run out, though. An iterative QAOA found only the first excited state at 36 qubits ([arXiv:2510.26859](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.26859)), and a routing QAOA had to drop from 20 layers to 8 on Forte ([arXiv:2602.04495](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.04495)).

The current releases are **qiskit-ionq 1.1.1** (June 5, 2026, requiring Qiskit 2.0 or later) and **Qiskit 2.5.2** (August 13, 2026) ([PyPI qiskit-ionq](https://pypi.org/project/qiskit-ionq/); [PyPI qiskit](https://pypi.org/project/qiskit/)). Code that uses `IonQProvider` with an API key does **not** carry over to Qollab, which runs its own Pyodide fork of qiskit-ionq and injects the backend for you. That only matters if you spend your own credits off-platform.

## The winning plan: small circuits, honest baselines, lavish documentation

### Before Friday: lock registration, rehearse the runtime, choose a lane

The next 24 hours are administrative. Fill in the GT form. Have the captain register the team on Qollab under the Georgia Tech node, and have every teammate sign in and accept before Thursday at 11:00 AM ET. If anyone on the team is not enrolled at Georgia Tech, register as professional; the prize pools are the same. The time before Friday 7 PM is best spent on skills that do not count as project code. Work through Qollab's six-lesson course. Publish a throwaway hello-world project so you know the publishing flow, the Run form and how backend injection works. Then write a one-paragraph plan that names the theme, the dataset or teaching topic, and the baseline you will compare against. PennyLane's QHack organizers give the most useful single piece of advice: settle what you are building and how before writing code, and check every day that you are still on-challenge ([PennyLane blog](https://pennylane.ai/blog/2024/02/how-to-do-a-great-hackathon-project)). Thursday's 7 PM James Weaver session includes hackathon project guidance ([GT site schedule](https://qhackathon-gt.lovable.app/#schedule)). The GT site says sponsors will release themes and problem statements on their own schedules, so read everything posted at kickoff before committing. Use the global Slack channel early to ask two things nobody has published: whether teams get any hardware credits during the build, and whether GT is adding local bonus prizes.

### Pick the theme where you can clear the bar honestly

**Quantum Impact** is closest to IonQ's commercial story: hybrid workflows like Airbus's aircraft-loading QAOA, which found optimal solutions from 12 to 28 qubits on Aria and Forte ([arXiv:2504.01567](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.01567)). It is also the hardest theme to score well on honestly. The brief requires a public dataset, a strong classical baseline and a measurable improvement ([Qollab Rules & terms](https://qollab.xyz/programs/hackathon/terms)). At 20 qubits, a decent classical solver will usually win. The way through is to report the comparison honestly, the way Quantum Courier does by pitting classical against quantum on five logistics problems and showing which wins ([Qollab Explore](https://qollab.xyz/explore)). Pair that with technical depth that shows you understand IonQ's hardware. One option is a fixed-schedule, linear-ramp QAOA. It has no variational loop, so a whole sweep fits in one IonQ job, and it has been tested on IonQ Aria ([arXiv:2405.09169](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.09169)). Use a dense problem (a QUBO) built from an industry that Qollab does not already cover, so that all-to-all connectivity matters. Count its two-qubit gates against an IBM heavy-hex compilation using Qollab's own IBM backends. Then apply feasibility post-selection, which IonQ recommends as a cheap fidelity gain ([IonQ best practices](https://ionq.com/best-practices)). Avoid quoting vendor speedups as proven. The Ansys claim of up to 12% faster processing came from simulating the hybrid algorithm ([The Quantum Insider](https://thequantuminsider.com/2025/03/20/ionqs-devices-boost-ansys-simulations-achieving-up-to-12-faster-processing-over-classical-computers/)). The AstraZeneca speedup of more than 20× was measured against earlier quantum benchmarks, not a purely classical baseline ([IonQ IR](https://investors.ionq.com/news/news-details/2025/IonQ-Speeds-Quantum-Accelerated-Drug-Development-Application-With-AstraZeneca-AWS-and-NVIDIA/default.aspx)).

**Quantum Unlocked** fits Qollab's taste best. QAVE won Qollab's first challenge as an educational visualizer, Documentation Quality rewards teaching naturally, and the theme's named topics (error correction, fault tolerance, networking, algorithms) overlap with IonQ's current interests. IonQ's 2026 iQuHACK challenge was entanglement distillation ([2026 IonQ challenge](https://github.com/iQuHACK/2026-IonQ)), and its acquisitions are concentrated in networking. A strong concept would let learners watch a protocol, such as Bell-pair distillation or [[4,2,2]] error detection, succeed or fail under IonQ Forte noise versus IBM Heron noise, with real numbers. It can include an original analytical twist. On IonQ hardware, [[4,2,2]] encoding helped on 11-qubit Harmony but showed no clear improvement on the lower-error Aria ([arXiv:2412.20736](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.20736)). Explaining why counts as the original analytical contribution that the tutorial bar accepts. Building on the qave library can save hours if you disclose it, and the lack of dynamic circuits means using post-selection or deferred measurement throughout.

**Quantum Playground** suits the precedents of Quantum Garden and iQuHACK's game winners. The bar is that interference, entanglement or measurement drives the experience, not dice rolls. Team 47's design, where the players are qubits, and Opti-maze's cooperative mode, where the player guides the quantum computer instead of competing with it ([IonQ blog](https://www.ionq.com/blog/february-09-2022-iquhack-winners-opti-maze)), are good templates. Qollab's `index.html` plus `main.js` path gives you a real interactive front end. Steer away from music unless you have a genuinely new mechanism, because that corner of the catalog is crowded. Playground's weakness is Impact Potential. Strengthen it with an educational framing that makes playing the game teach something precise.

My position: **for a typical student team, Unlocked, or a Playground entry with a rigorous technical core, offers the highest expected value**. Choose Impact only if someone on the team can build and defend a credible classical baseline within the first 16 hours.

### Engineer for the judge's Run click

The single most avoidable failure is a project that hangs, errors or needs setup when a judge presses Run. The default run should take no input, finish in seconds on the built-in simulator, use well under 24 qubits, and send everything as **one batched job** so it also runs on IonQ's remote simulator. Read the injected backend's capacity instead of hardcoding one. Fix random seeds. Ship datasets and precomputed IonQ-noise results as project files within the 2 MB-per-file limit, and handle a failed GET request gracefully. Typed `main()` parameters with sensible defaults give judges an optional form without blocking the default path. Test once on the IonQ Forte remote noise-model simulator to confirm you comply with the one-job rule. Then run the project from a fresh account or private browser window, as a stranger would. Any hardware run you manage is unscored, but it can still strengthen the story. If you do one, keep debiasing on at 500 or more shots and show both the averaged and sharpened results from the same job ([IonQ Docs: Debiasing](https://docs.ionq.com/features/error-mitigation-debiasing)).

### Make IonQ fluency visible

IonQ representatives sit on every local panel, and past winners earned credit for using trapped-ion features ([Entanglement5 PR](https://github.com/iQuHACK/2023_IonQ/pull/3)). Put the evidence on the page. Report qubit counts, compiled two-qubit gate counts and shots. Show that RZZ becomes one native ZZ gate on Forte and that all-to-all connectivity removes SWAPs. Show results side by side: ideal, Forte noise model, mitigated, and classical. If you use zero-noise extrapolation, include a control. A 2026 study found that a deliberately flawed noise-scaling control produced a *larger* apparent improvement than real noise scaling ([arXiv:2607.09360](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.09360)). Close with a short, honest limitations section that claims no quantum advantage and explains where noise breaks the method. That is the critical thinking QHack's organizers say can outscore flashier work ([PennyLane blog](https://pennylane.ai/blog/2024/02/how-to-do-a-great-hackathon-project)).

### Run a 48-hour clock with an early safety submission

The schedule below adapts a common hackathon pattern to Qollab's rules. You can edit until the deadline and rough projects remain eligible ([Qollab FAQ](https://qollab.xyz/programs/hackathon/how-it-works#faq)), so a runnable submission on Saturday night costs nothing and protects against Sunday disasters. Mentors are live Saturday and Sunday in areas including QAOA and variational methods, error mitigation, chemistry, finance, optimization and tooling, so book them for the noise and mitigation phase ([Qollab Mentors](https://qollab.xyz/programs/hackathon/mentors)).

| Clock (ET) | Hours | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| Fri 7 PM – 11 PM | H0–4 | Read kickoff materials; finalize problem statement, success metric, theme, dataset and baseline; assign roles |
| Fri 11 PM – Sat 11 AM | H4–16 | Classical baseline plus an ideal-simulator prototype at 4–12 qubits, already running on Qollab |
| Sat 11 AM – 7 PM | H16–24 | Forte versus IBM noise sweeps, client-side mitigation, scaling study; mentor sessions; **freeze scope at H24** |
| Sat 7 PM – Sun 7 AM | H24–36 | Interactive UI or visualizations, project page draft; **submit a rough but runnable version** |
| Sun 7 AM – 3 PM | H36–44 | Documentation polish, 2–3 minute video, accessibility pass, fresh-account Run test |
| Sun 3 PM – 7 PM | H44–48 | Check the public video link, attribution line and disclosures; final submit by about 5 PM |

A four-person team splits naturally into four roles: a circuit lead (ansatz design, compilation, two-qubit gate count), a baseline and data lead, a noise and benchmarking lead (sweeps, mitigation, error bars), and a product and communication lead (UI, project page, video). The five-project allowance is a real but double-edged lever. A second, smaller entry aimed at a different theme gives a second shot at the GT top three and a specialty nomination. But it must clear the tutorial bar on its own, must not duplicate the flagship, and should start only after the flagship is frozen.

### Spend documentation effort like it is a quarter of the score

It is a quarter of the score, and the Best Documentation award adds a second payoff. A winning project page opens with the problem and why it matters. It explains the quantum mechanism and why it is essential, gives one-click run instructions, and presents results with their baselines. It then covers limitations, a way for others to replicate or extend the work, and full disclosure of AI use, forks and anything created before the window. It ends with the required Qollab and IonQ attribution line. QHack's organizers ask for a README that explains every file, so judges are never left doing code archaeology ([PennyLane blog](https://pennylane.ai/blog/2024/02/how-to-do-a-great-hackathon-project)). The video should show the Run click and the key result within the first 30 seconds. Accessibility is a stated submission requirement, not a nicety ([Qollab Rules & terms](https://qollab.xyz/programs/hackathon/terms)).

## Conclusion

This contest flips the usual quantum-hackathon hierarchy. At iQuHACK, the team with the best hardware run had an edge. Here hardware is unscored, judging happens in a browser tab, and a quarter of the rubric is documentation, so the competition is really about **software craft and explanation constrained by IonQ physics**. A student team with no credits is not at a disadvantage. The biggest risks are things you control: missing Thursday's registration, a project stuck at tutorial level that gets zeroed on half the rubric, or a Run click that fails. Because the GT node is new and may be small, the step that decides everything else is reaching Georgia Tech's top three or becoming its nominee for a specialty award, and that is far more attainable than the global headline prize. The six specialty awards add parallel paths, and Documentation is the one you control most directly.

The payoff also continues past October 11. A well-documented, MIT-licensed Qollab project can feed straight into Qollab's rolling Grant Program, worth up to US$20,000 in IonQ credits, or a Fellows invitation. For students, the global top three's fast track to IonQ interviews may be worth more than the cash. Five questions remain open until kickoff and Slack: the rubric weights, the judges' identities, the GT node's size, any build-time hardware credits, and whether IonQ adds problem statements at 7 PM Friday. Check all five when the window opens, and build so the answers do not change your plan.
